# Fight The Future: consultancy business plan

**Version:** 1.0  
**Date:** 26 August 2026  
**Launch-readiness target:** 26 September 2026  
**Planning horizon:** 24 months  
**Operating route:** University of Liverpool institutional consultancy through CONSULT

## Executive summary

Fight The Future will be a small, premium event consultancy built around live, narrative-driven experiences that teach doctoral researchers how to work critically and collaboratively with agent-based coding AI. The flagship event already exists: participants enter a fictional crisis, use coding agents to solve personalised data challenges, verify executable outputs and combine their individual answers to progress. It is short, memorable and difficult to reproduce with a lecture or generic AI demonstration.

The initial buyers are Centres for Doctoral Training (CDTs), Doctoral Training Partnerships and university researcher-development teams. They need cohort-building activities, responsible AI development, transferable skills and practical digital training. The initial proposition is not “learn to code in two hours.” It is a facilitated experience in which researchers practise specifying problems, running and inspecting generated code, checking results, coordinating with peers and retaining responsibility for AI-supported work.

For the first 24 months, the venture should remain a University of Liverpool institutional consultancy rather than becoming a separate company. This provides contracting, invoicing, insurance and pricing support while the offer is validated. One flagship product should be sold before a wider event suite is built.

The core commercial package will be priced at **£5,000 plus VAT**, with travel and accommodation charged separately at cost. A tightly controlled founding-partner delivery may be offered at **£3,500 plus VAT**. The base plan is one pilot and seven flagship events over two years. Subject to CONSULT confirming the assumed full economic cost, this produces about **£10,213 cumulative personal take-home** on a current £70,000 salary.

The immediate objective is to become ready to sell and scope work within one month. Success at day 30 means that the institutional route, intellectual property position, price, delivery specification and accessibility baseline are confirmed; the event has passed a dry run; a client-facing one-page proposition exists; at least 25 prospects are identified; and the first three discovery conversations are booked or completed.

## 1. Purpose and ambition

### 1.1 Mission

To give researchers a vivid, practical experience of collaborating with people and coding agents while preserving human judgement, verification and accountability.

### 1.2 Twenty-four-month objective

This plan interprets “£10k take-home after two years” as **at least £10,000 cumulative personal take-home from consultancy payments during the first 24 months**. This is deliberately modest enough to validate demand without allowing consultancy to overwhelm the founder's University role.

The target is:

- eight paid external events in 24 months;
- at least £38,500 revenue excluding VAT and pass-through travel;
- at least £20,250 net consultancy income made available for payroll;
- approximately £10,213 cumulative personal take-home;
- at least two repeat bookings, referrals or multi-event commissions; and
- a defensible decision at month 18 on whether to expand the suite, license the format or remain a specialist flagship provider.

### 1.3 Strategic boundaries

For the first year, Fight The Future will:

- sell one repeatable flagship experience;
- target UK doctoral-training and researcher-development buyers;
- operate institutionally through Liverpool CONSULT;
- use synthetic or scenario data, never client-sensitive research data;
- make careful design and participant-experience claims, not unsupported learning-impact claims; and
- add bespoke content only where the additional fee covers its full cost.

It will not initially become a broad AI-training agency, build multiple speculative products, offer open public ticketing or promise competence certification.

## 2. The opportunity

### 2.1 Buyer need

Doctoral programmes are expected to develop researchers as well as fund individual research projects. Cohort programmes need activities that can serve mixed disciplines and uneven technical starting points while producing meaningful interaction. Meanwhile, coding agents have made it easier to generate plausible code and more important to inspect, execute, test and take responsibility for it.

The relevant institutional demand is visible:

- UKRI announced more than **£1 billion across 65 EPSRC CDTs**, with major university and partner contributions and training in nationally important technologies, including AI, engineering biology, semiconductors and telecommunications.
- UKRI's current doctoral focal-award portfolio spans digital systems, health, energy, manufacturing, materials and other fields where data and computational practice matter.
- The Researcher Development Concordat establishes an expectation of professional development, commonly expressed as ten days per year for researchers.
- Jisc's student research reports demand for clear, practical AI guidance and learning that combines human support, discussion and collaboration with AI use.

This does not prove that a buyer will purchase Fight The Future. It does show a funded and identifiable market with an ongoing reason to commission cohort development and responsible digital-skills activity.

### 2.2 Initial customer segments

The priority order is:

1. **CDTs and doctoral focal awards with data-rich but not exclusively computing cohorts.** Their participants have an immediate reason to use code, but coding confidence is likely to vary.
2. **Doctoral colleges and researcher-development academies.** They can commission cross-faculty activity and may offer repeat cohorts.
3. **Doctoral Training Partnerships and industrial doctoral programmes.** The collaboration and human-accountability proposition aligns well with interdisciplinary and partner-facing training.
4. **Research institutes, learned societies and early-career researcher programmes.** These become a secondary market after two external CDT references exist.

Pure computer-science and AI CDTs are not the first target. Their participants may find the coding difficulty too low unless a specialist variant is developed. The strongest early fit is a mixed cohort that values AI literacy, confidence, collaboration and verification more than advanced syntax.

### 2.3 Buying roles

The economic buyer is normally a CDT director, centre manager, training lead, doctoral-college lead or researcher-development manager. A cohort coordinator or academic champion may initiate the conversation. Procurement and finance enable the purchase; local IT, room-booking and accessibility teams affect delivery.

The sale must therefore answer four different questions:

- **Director:** Why is this worth part of the cohort-training budget?
- **Training lead:** What will participants actually do and how does it fit our programme?
- **Operations/IT:** What room, hardware, accounts and support are required?
- **Procurement:** What is included, who contracts, and what will it cost?

## 3. The offer

### 3.1 Flagship: Fight The Future

Fight The Future is a browser-supported, facilitated coding mission lasting approximately two hours, normally embedded in a half-day client package. Participants use an agent in Visual Studio Code to create or repair executable R, Python or JavaScript files. Across four stages they work with personalised datasets and produce individual six-digit results. A private checker validates each result, while a shared podium advances only valid stage-aligned groups. Required collaboration increases through the experience.

The package is designed around five learning actions:

1. turn a problem specification into an agent request;
2. inspect the files and code produced by the agent;
3. execute the work locally and diagnose failure;
4. verify an apparently plausible result; and
5. coordinate individual outputs into a shared result.

The distinctive mechanism is the separation of **personal correctness** from **collective progression**. Every participant must produce a valid personal output, but nobody completes the later stages entirely alone.

### 3.2 Client outcome

The buyer receives:

- a structured cohort experience rather than a passive AI seminar;
- a practical introduction to agent-supported coding using synthetic data;
- visible practice in verification, collaboration and accountability;
- a facilitated debrief connecting the fiction to doctoral research practice;
- an operational event report with attendance and system-level completion information; and
- an optional follow-up discussion on adapting the principles to the centre's training programme.

Claims must remain proportionate. Until an appropriate evaluation exists, materials should say the event is **designed to develop or prompt** these practices, not that it has been proven to improve them.

### 3.3 Standard package

**Price: £5,000 plus VAT. Travel and accommodation are charged separately at cost.**

Included:

- one 45-minute client discovery and configuration meeting;
- one technical-readiness meeting or written site check;
- standard branding with the client's cohort name and agreed welcome text;
- participant pre-arrival instructions;
- one live delivery for up to 40 participants;
- facilitator dashboard, hosted application and offline contingency pack;
- two-hour experience plus introduction, break/reset allowance and debrief;
- lightweight operational evaluation and a short sponsor report; and
- a 30-minute post-event review.

Excluded or separately priced:

- new scenario writing or discipline-specific datasets;
- integration with client systems;
- additional facilitators required by cohort size or accessibility needs;
- participant travel, catering, room hire or hardware;
- formal educational evaluation or research; and
- delivery outside the UK.

### 3.4 Founding-partner offer

One external founding partner may receive the standard event for **£3,500 plus VAT**, provided that:

- it is delivered using the standard scenario with minimal customisation;
- the date fits the founder's availability;
- the client participates in a structured sponsor debrief;
- non-identifiable operational facts and an approved testimonial may be used in future marketing; and
- the CONSULT costing confirms that the delivery remains financially sensible.

The discount is an evidence-acquisition cost and must not become the reference price.

### 3.5 Future suite

Only after three paid deliveries and clear buyer demand should two adjacent products be developed:

- **Trust the Output?** A 90-minute verification lab in which teams inspect, test and repair plausible but flawed agent-generated analyses.
- **Mission Lab.** A half- or full-day bespoke event in which a CDT's domain becomes the setting for agent-supported, collaborative problem solving.

The flagship remains the lead product. New offers must reuse the platform, facilitation model and operational assets rather than create an unrelated consultancy burden.

## 4. Positioning and competitive advantage

### 4.1 Positioning statement

For doctoral programmes seeking practical AI literacy and meaningful cohort interaction, Fight The Future is a live, facilitated coding mission that makes participants execute and verify agent-generated work and depend on one another to progress. Unlike a lecture, generic prompt workshop or open-ended hackathon, it combines personalised data, deterministic checking, escalating collaboration and a complete narrative arc in a controlled session.

### 4.2 Alternatives and differentiation

| Alternative | Strength | Gap addressed by Fight The Future |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI lecture or webinar | Scales cheaply; easy to schedule | Participants must produce, run and verify an artifact |
| Generic prompt workshop | Practical and accessible | Coding output is executable and deterministically checked |
| Hackathon | Authentic and open-ended | The event is bounded, facilitated and designed to finish |
| Coding course | Builds deeper technical skill | The experience serves mixed starting points in one session |
| Escape room/team-building event | Memorable and social | Collaboration is tied to computational outputs and AI accountability |

The defensible assets are not the crisis story alone. They are the tested platform, dataset-generation system, personal checker, podium matching logic, facilitator controls, contingency generator, delivery knowledge and the design principles connecting them.

## 5. Commercial model

### 5.1 Route to market

The first 24 months should use the **institutional CONSULT route**:

- the University contracts with and invoices the client;
- the work receives University insurance and indemnity cover;
- Research & Finance supplies FEC and non-FEC costing;
- the Consultancy Team supports pricing and contract negotiation; and
- approved net income can be retained, paid through payroll or split.

A separate company is unnecessary at validation stage and could create avoidable questions about University IP, resources, insurance and conflicts of interest. The private-consultancy route remains a future option only after written approval and a deliberate comparison of risk, tax, ownership and administrative effort.

### 5.2 Pricing rule

Every quote must be built from:

> **Price excluding VAT = confirmed CONSULT FEC + direct project costs + required payroll fund + risk margin**

At a £70,000 salary, and assuming no student-loan or other exceptional deductions, £1 of net consultancy income made available for payroll produces approximately £0.504 of personal take-home under 2026/27 rates. Consequently:

> **Required payroll fund = desired personal take-home ÷ 0.50435**

For example, a desired £1,250 take-home requires about £2,478 of payroll funds. If confirmed FEC and direct delivery costs are £2,250, the price floor is approximately £4,728 excluding VAT. The commercial price should therefore be £5,000, not £4,728.

### 5.3 Price discipline

- Quote all UK prices **excluding VAT**, stating that VAT will be added where applicable.
- Charge travel and accommodation separately at cost so geography does not consume the event margin.
- Do not discount below the founding-partner price.
- Do not include bespoke datasets within the standard package.
- Re-cost the service after any material change in delivery time, staffing, platform cost or University policy.
- Review the headline price annually and whenever two consecutive proposals close without price resistance.

## 6. Financial plan

### 6.1 Tax and payroll assumptions

The model assumes:

- current salary of £70,000;
- England, Wales and Northern Ireland 2026/27 higher-rate income tax of 40%;
- employee National Insurance at 2% on incremental earnings above the upper earnings limit;
- employer National Insurance at 15%, paid from the consultancy funds;
- no pension deduction, consistent with the CONSULT payroll form;
- no student-loan, postgraduate-loan, benefit or other payroll deductions;
- all relevant additional income remains below £100,000 in each tax year; and
- VAT and reimbursed travel are excluded from revenue and surplus.

For each £1,000 available for payroll:

- gross consultancy pay is about £869.57;
- employer NI is about £130.43;
- income tax is about £347.83;
- employee NI is about £17.39; and
- personal take-home is about **£504.35**.

### 6.2 Base-case unit economics

The following is a planning case, not an approved University costing:

| Item | Founding partner | Standard event |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Fee excluding VAT | £3,500 | £5,000 |
| Assumed CONSULT FEC and direct costs | £2,500 | £2,250 |
| Net income available for payroll | £1,000 | £2,750 |
| Estimated personal take-home | £504 | £1,387 |
| Take-home as percentage of fee | 14.4% | 27.7% |

The founding event costs more because it includes additional preparation, operational refinement and evidence capture. Repeated standard delivery must become more efficient, while any client-specific development is separately priced.

The financial model is valid only if the first CONSULT costing returns standard-event FEC and direct costs at or below **£2,250**. If it is higher, the headline price, included work or take-home target must change.

| Period | Mix | Revenue ex VAT | Assumed FEC/direct costs | Payroll fund | Estimated take-home |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |

- reduce repeat-event preparation time and therefore FEC;

| Base | 8 | £2,531 | £10,213 |

The downside case is not a failed business if it produces strong evidence and repeat demand, but it misses the personal-income objective. The trigger for intervention is fewer than three booked or delivered paid events by the end of month 12.

## 7. Sales and marketing plan

### 7.1 Message

Lead with the buyer's problem, not the platform:

> Your researchers are already encountering AI-generated code. Fight The Future gives a mixed-experience cohort a safe, memorable way to practise directing an agent, running and checking its work, and coordinating with other people—within one facilitated mission.

Supporting messages:

- practical rather than lecture-based;
- appropriate for mixed coding experience;
- designed around human accountability;
- useful as both AI-literacy and cohort-development activity;
- synthetic data and a controlled technical environment; and
- delivered through the University of Liverpool.

Avoid phrases such as “proven to improve collaboration,” “learn coding in two hours” or “AI-proof your researchers.”

### 7.2 Prospecting strategy

Begin with warm and near-warm institutional relationships, while separating internal validation from external sales:

1. University of Liverpool CDTs, Doctoral College and researcher-development contacts for introductions, feedback and non-revenue validation;
2. externally led collaborating universities and centres connected to Liverpool academics for the first paid work;
3. data-rich external CDTs in bioscience, health, environment, chemistry, engineering and materials;
4. multi-institution doctoral partnerships needing an annual cohort event; and
5. national researcher-development networks after two external references exist.

Internal Liverpool activity should not be counted in the revenue plan unless Finance and CONSULT explicitly confirm that it can generate approved personal consultancy income. The eight-event financial target assumes external clients invoiced by the University.

Build a named list containing organisation, programme, director, manager/training lead, cohort size, training calendar, warm route, likely need and next action. The first list should contain 25 accounts, not hundreds of generic email addresses.

### 7.3 Funnel targets

Over 24 months:

- 60 qualified named accounts;
- 40 substantive buyer conversations;
- 16 written proposals;
- eight paid bookings;
- proposal-to-booking conversion of at least 50%; and
- at least two repeat, referral or multi-cohort bookings.

For the first month:

- 25 named prospects;
- ten tailored introductions or approaches;
- three discovery conversations;
- one formal pilot opportunity; and
- one proposal ready to enter CONSULT.

### 7.4 Discovery questions

Every first conversation should establish:

1. What AI, coding or cohort-development training is already provided?
2. Where do participants struggle: access, confidence, verification, disciplinary relevance or collaboration?
3. What cohort size, timing and technical environment must be supported?
4. What would make the event valuable enough to repeat?
5. Who owns the budget, procurement and final decision?
6. Are there accessibility, account, data-protection or AI-policy requirements that alter delivery?

The goal of discovery is qualification and adaptation of the proposition—not free design consultancy.

### 7.5 Proof assets

By the first external sale, create:

- a one-page buyer proposition;
- a 60–90 second screen recording or five-slide visual walkthrough;
- a technical-readiness sheet;
- a sample agenda;
- facilitator biography and University affiliation;
- an evidence-safe account of the original Liverpool delivery;
- a clear statement of intended outcomes and evidence limitations; and
- a sponsor report template.

## 8. Delivery and operations

### 8.1 Standard delivery workflow

**Four to six weeks before:** confirm scope, contract, date, room, cohort size, accessibility needs, client AI policy, devices, connectivity and accounts.

**Two weeks before:** issue participant preflight instructions; confirm browser, VS Code, runtime and agent access; generate the run and offline packs; agree the client escalation contact.

**Two days before:** complete platform health check, backup export, facilitator rehearsal and final attendance assumptions; prepare collaboration caps and fallback rules.

**On the day:** technical orientation, mission briefing, four stages, controlled interventions, debrief and sponsor handover.

**Within five working days:** provide operational report, capture lessons, record reusable improvements, request testimonial/referral where appropriate and initiate payroll only after client payment has been received.

### 8.2 Capacity

The initial standard offer is capped at 40 participants with one lead facilitator, subject to the dry run and risk assessment. Cohorts of 41–60 require a trained second facilitator and revised price. Larger cohorts should be declined until the format has been tested at scale.

Because voluntary registrations may not translate into attendance, collaboration requirements must be configurable. The client should provide a realistic attendance estimate shortly before delivery, and the facilitator should retain authority to reduce group thresholds to preserve finishability.

### 8.3 Reliability

Every event requires:

- a tested hosted instance and fresh event run;
- deterministic solution checks;
- downloadable individual data;
- a complete offline contingency pack and facilitator answer sheet;
- a local copy of participant instructions;
- a documented pause/reset/recovery procedure;
- a method for participants without working agent access; and
- a named venue IT contact.

The product is a live experience. Operational resilience is part of what the client buys.

### 8.4 Accessibility and inclusion

Before external sale, the delivery design should include:

- advance disclosure that collaboration is central;
- structured roles such as prompter, code reader, runner and verifier;
- facilitator-mediated or text-based partner finding;
- a route to participate without moving publicly around the room;
- keyboard, contrast, reduced-motion and screen-reader checks;
- accessible pre-arrival materials;
- breaks and clear timing; and
- a process for agreeing reasonable adjustments with the client.

The aim is to preserve interdependence without making public confidence or physical mobility a hidden prerequisite.

### 8.5 Evaluation

Standard delivery will collect only the information needed to operate and improve the service:

- registrations and attendance supplied by the client;
- non-identifiable stage progression and completion timing;
- system errors, overrides and facilitator interventions;
- short participant reaction questions; and
- sponsor assessment of fit, value and likelihood to rebook.

Operational reporting must not be presented as proof of learning. Any research using participant behaviour, experience, submissions or transfer tasks requires the appropriate ethical review and informed consent before data collection.

## 9. Governance and CONSULT requirements

### 9.1 Actions required before sale

- Obtain and retain written line-manager/approver support.
- Ask the Consultancy Team to confirm that the activity is consultancy/CPD and that the institutional route is correct.
- Submit a representative standard event for CONSULT costing, including honest preparation, administration, delivery and follow-up time.
- Confirm any School or departmental local policy variation affecting income allocation.
- Confirm the current definition of “net income” and whether FEC is recovered before payroll allocation.
- Confirm that a personal payroll allocation is approved in principle.
- Confirm the intellectual-property ownership and right to commercialise the platform, narrative, datasets and brand.
- Confirm hosting, software licensing and permitted use of University resources for external delivery.
- Obtain current VAT advice; the supplied guidance treats bespoke courses and UK consultancy as normally subject to 20% VAT.
- Do not agree scope, price or terms contractually before the appropriate University approval.

### 9.2 Payroll treatment

The supplied CONSULT guidance states that institutional net income may be retained in a cost centre, paid through payroll or split, subject to approval and local variations. The payroll request value includes employer NI; payroll then deducts employer NI, income tax and employee NI. The form states that pension is not payable on this consultancy payment.

Payroll should be requested only once the client has paid and all project liabilities are known. Retaining a proportion of early surplus in a G cost centre may be prudent if hosting, accessibility work or product maintenance requires investment.

### 9.3 Intellectual property and brand

The software and event were developed in a University context and have already been delivered internally. Ownership must therefore be resolved in writing, not assumed. The required answer is not merely who owns the source code; it must cover:

- the Fight The Future name and visual identity;
- the application, dataset generators and contingency tools;
- narrative and participant materials;
- future client-specific adaptations;
- permission to host and demonstrate the platform; and
- what a client may retain, reuse or modify after delivery.

Until this is confirmed, sell facilitated access to an event—not ownership or a transferable software licence.

### Week 2: productise the delivery — 3 to 9 September

- Convert the existing event into a repeatable runbook with named timings and decisions.
- Complete a technical-readiness sheet and participant preflight guide.
- Test the hosted deployment, contingency pack and reset/recovery workflow.
- Add the accessibility baseline and alternative collaboration route.
- Draft the one-page proposition, sample agenda, proposal template and sponsor report.
- Define exactly what “standard customisation” permits.

**Gate:** A colleague unfamiliar with the build should be able to understand the client journey and delivery requirements from the pack.

### Week 3: validate the market — 10 to 16 September

- Build the first 25-account prospect list.
- Request ten warm introductions or send ten tailored approaches.
- Run at least three discovery conversations.
- Test the proposition, price, timing, cohort size and procurement assumptions.
- Record objections verbatim and revise the offer only where a pattern appears.
- Identify one credible founding-partner opportunity.

**Gate:** At least one buyer should be willing to discuss a date, budget or written proposal. Interest without a next step is not validation.

### Week 4: prove readiness — 17 to 26 September

- Run a full timed rehearsal with 8–16 representative participants or testers.
- Simulate account failure, low attendance, uneven progress and loss of connectivity.
- Fix only launch-critical defects and record later enhancements separately.
- Finalise the risk assessment, delivery checklist and client documents.
- Agree the first proposal and CONSULT/contract workflow.
- Set a delivery calendar with protected preparation and travel capacity.

**Gate:** The consultancy is launch-ready when it can be safely sold, scoped and scheduled. The first client event may occur later because university contracting and client procurement can take several weeks.

## 12. Principal risks and controls

| Risk | Early warning | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CONSULT FEC makes £5,000 uneconomic | Standard cost exceeds £2,250 | Increase price, reduce included time or change income goal before selling |
| University IP position prevents commercial use | Ownership cannot be confirmed | Resolve with the appropriate University team before external marketing |
| Buyer sees it as entertainment rather than development | Questions focus only on fun or prizes | Lead with learning actions, debrief and sponsor report |
| Mixed technical setup consumes the event | Failed preflight or unclear client IT ownership | Mandatory readiness check, offline packs and account-failure route |
| Attendance makes group sizes impossible | Late attendance falls below plan | Configurable caps, stable teams and facilitator fallback |
| Mandatory collaboration excludes participants | Adjustment requests or public partner-finding anxiety | Accessible roles, mediated matching and non-mobile participation route |
| Agent-generated code is accepted uncritically | Fast completion without inspection | Add explicit verification prompts and debrief questions |
| Bespoke requests destroy repeatability | Proposal contains new story, platform or data work | Separate scope and price; say no when reuse is low |
| Sales cycle is longer than expected | Positive calls but no procurement step | Start with warm networks and calendar-led cohort needs; maintain pipeline |
| Evidence claims exceed what was evaluated | Marketing language says “improves” or “proves” | Use design-intent language and pre-plan any formal evaluation |
| Consultancy conflicts with core employment | Preparation expands into work commitments | Limit event volume, agree workload boundaries and review quarterly |

## 13. Decision rules

Proceed with the £5,000 package only if:

- institutional delivery and IP use are approved;
- CONSULT indicates standard-event FEC/direct cost of no more than £2,250, or a higher price is accepted;
- the September dry run is operationally successful;
- at least one qualified buyer accepts that the proposition addresses a real programme need; and
- delivery can fit University duties without relying on hidden unpaid labour.

Review the strategy if:

- no proposal-quality opportunity exists after 20 qualified conversations;
- the first two proposals both fail primarily on price;
- repeat preparation remains above the cost assumption after two deliveries;
- buyers consistently request a different format; or
- delivery evidence shows that collaboration or technical access is causing avoidable exclusion.

Expand the event suite only if:

- three paid events have been delivered;
- the flagship runbook is stable;
- at least two buyers request a recognisably similar second product; and
- development can be funded through a confirmed commission or retained consultancy income.
